FitzHugh-Nagumo model

Consider the FHN system: \[ \left\{\begin{aligned} \varepsilon v' &= f(v) - w + I_\text{app}, \\ w' &= v - \gamma w, \end{aligned}\right. \]

with \(f(v) = v(1-v)(v-\alpha)\) and \(I_\text{app} \in \mathbb{R}\). We assume that \(\varepsilon \ll 1\), so that we can always apply the singular perturbation analysis.

  1. Show that for \(I_\text{app}>0\) constant and sufficiently large (but not too large), the system has a (singular) limit cycle. Provide a formula for the period.

  2. Try to sketch the bifurcation diagram with respect to \(I_\text{app}\), mentioning all bifurcations.

  3. Anode break excitation is when the potential is slowly decreased (thus, \(I_\text{app}\) is negative rather than positive) and then switched off. Can we trigger an action potential in this way? Does it work also when the application time of the current is short?

Roger-McCulloch model

Consider the Roger-McCulloch system: \[ \left\{\begin{aligned} \varepsilon v' &= -kv(v-\alpha)(v-1) - vw + I_\text{app}, \\ w' &= v - \gamma w, \end{aligned}\right. \]

Suppose \(\varepsilon \ll 1\). The parameters have the same meaning as in the FitzHugh-Nagumo system.

  1. Consider \(I_\text{app}=0\). Study the phase portrait and show how an action potential may occur.

  2. What does it happen when \(I_\text{app}\neq 0\)? Is the situation similar to the previous exercise?

Hodgkin-Huxley model

When using the voltage-clamp technique, the total transmembrane current can be accurately measured. However, Hodgkin and Huxley were interested in the individual sodium \(I_\ce{Na}\) and potassium \(I_{K}\) currents. How can we estimate them from \(I_\text{tot} = I_\ce{Na} + I_\ce{K}\)? This exercise addresses the question.

  1. Are there potential levels (fixed by clamping) at which we can measure a pure $ I_$ or \(I_\ce{K}\) current?
Solution

Yes, at the Nernst potentials. When \(V=V_\ce{Na}\), \(I_\ce{Na} = 0\) so \(I_\text{tot} = I_\ce{K}\), and when \(V=V_\ce{K}\), \(I_\ce{K}=0\) so \(I_\text{tot} = I_\ce{Na}\). Of course, in this case we only get the current for a specific value of \(V\), and not for all values, so we cannot use it to determine the gating dynamics.

  1. Suppose 90% of the \(\ce{Na+}\) in the bath of a squid axon is replaced by inert choline, preserving electroneutrality. What happens to the equilibrium potentials?
Solution

Only \(V_\ce{Na}\) is affected. From the Nernst equation: \[ V^1_\ce{Na} = \frac{RT}{zF}\ln\frac{\ce{[Na+]^1_e}}{\ce{[Na+]^1_i}} \]

where we use “1” to reference quantities (no choline).

If w replace 90% of the extracellular sodium, then \(\ce{[Na+]^2_e} = 0.1\ce{[Na+]^1_e}\) and \(\ce{[Na+]^1_i}=\ce{[Na+]^2_i}\) so we have: \[ \begin{split} V^2_\ce{Na} &= \frac{RT}{zF}\ln\frac{\ce{[Na+]^2_e}}{\ce{[Na+]^2_i}} \\ &= \frac{RT}{zF}\ln\frac{0.1\ce{[Na+]^1_e}}{\ce{[Na+]^1_i}} \\ &= V^1_\ce{Na} - \frac{RT}{zF}\ln 10 \end{split} \]

With the actual numbers (see the Table 12.1), \(V^2_\ce{Na} \approx -3.48\,\text{mV}\).

  1. Explain why replacing the extracellular \(\ce{Na+}\) with choline has little effect on the resting potential of an axon. Calculate the new resting potential with 90% of the extracellular \(\ce{Na+}\) removed. Why is the same not true if \(\ce{K+}\) is replaced? Assume \(m = 0.0532\), \(n = 0.3184\), \(h=0.5945\) (constants), \(\bar{g}_\ce{Na} = 120\,\text{mS}\), \(\bar{g}_\ce{K} = 36\,\text{mS}\), \(\bar{g}_\text{leak} =0.3\,\text{mS}\), \(V_\text{leak} = -54.4\,\text{mV}\) and \(V_\ce{K} = -77.3\,\text{mV}\).
Solution

Using the formula: \[ V_\text{eq} = \frac{g_\ce{Na}V_\ce{Na}+g_\ce{K}V_\ce{K}+g_l V_l}{g_\ce{Na}+g_\ce{K}+g_l} \]

with \(g_\ce{Na} = \bar{g}_\ce{Na} m^3 h\), \(g_\ce{K} = \bar{g}_\ce{K} n^4\). With \(V_\ce{Na} = V_\ce{Na}^1\) we obtain \(V^1_\text{eq} \approx -65\,\text{mV}\), with \(V_\ce{Na}^2\) we have \(V^2_\text{eq} \approx -66\,\text{mV}\).

The reason is that while \(V_\ce{Na}\) changes significantly, most of the channels are closed because \(m\approx 0\), so they contribute little to the resting membrane potential.

If we change the extracellular \(\ce{K+}\), then \(V^2_\ce{K} \approx -136\,\text{mV}\) and the new resting membrane potential would be \(V^2_\text{rest}\approx -136\,\text{mV}\). In fact, \(n\neq 0\), so potassium channels contribute the most to the resting membrane potential.

  1. Suppose we know \(K = I^1_\ce{Na}/I^2_\ce{Na}\), where \(I^1_\ce{Na}\) is the normal sodium current and \(I^2_\ce{Na}\) is the one obtained with 90% of extracellular sodium replaced by choline. Suppose also that \(K\) does not change over time and \(I^1_\ce{K}=I^2_\ce{K}\). Find \(I^1_\ce{Na}\) and \(I^2_\ce{Na}\) from \(I^1_\text{tot}\) and \(I^2_\text{tot}\). (A possible way to measure \(K\) is to look at \(I_\ce{tot}\) immediately after the voltage clamp, because most of the initial current is carried by sodium.)
Solution

Since \(I^1_\text{tot} = I^1_\ce{Na} + I^1\ce{K}\) and \(I^2_\text{tot} = I^2_\ce{Na} + I^2\ce{K}\), we solve the system: \[ \begin{aligned} I^1_\text{tot} &= I^1_\ce{Na} + I^1\ce{K}, \\ I^2_\text{tot} &= 1/K I^1_\ce{Na} + I^1\ce{K}. \end{aligned} \]

The solution is: \[ \begin{aligned} I^1_\ce{Na} &= \frac{K}{1-K}(I^2_\text{tot} - I^1_\text{tot}), \\ I^1_\ce{K} &= \frac{KI^2_\text{tot}- I^1_\text{tot}}{1-K}. \end{aligned} \]

  1. Nowadays a simpler way to block channels is with drugs. Tetrodotoxin (TTX) is a powerful neurotoxin, famous for the puffer fish. TTX acts by blocking the sodium channels, hence making them unavailable for action potential formation.

    Here is how we can model it. If \(P_0\) represents the total number of \(\ce{Na+}\) channels, and we denote by \(\ce{P}\) and \(\ce{B}\) respectively the number of non-blocked and blocked channels, then the reaction is \[ \ce{P + TTX <=>[k_{-1}][k_1] B}. \]

    Modify the sodium conductance to account for the presence of .

Solution

With the quasi-equilibrium approximation, knowing that \(\ce{[P]}+\ce{[B]}=\ce{[P]_0}\), we have: \[ \frac{P}{P_0} = \frac{1}{1 + \ce{TTX}/K_1}. \]

where \(K_1 = \frac{k_{-1}}{k_1}\).

The maximum sodium conductance is proportional to the number of available channels. Hence, \[ \bar{g}_\ce{Na}(\ce{TTX}) = \frac{\bar{g}^\text{max}_\ce{Na}}{1 + \ce{TTX}/K_1}. \]

Alternans

  1. Consider now \(I_\text{app}(t)\) as a square pulse of duration and amplitude sufficiently large to trigger an action potential (AP). The duration of the stimulus is negligible to the overall duration of the AP. Show that the AP duration (the excited phase) \(T_e\) and the recovery time \(T_r\) are: \[ \begin{aligned} T_e(w_0) &= \int_{w_0}^{w^*} \frac{\mathrm{d}w}{V_+(w)-\gamma w}, \\ T_e(w_0) &= \int_{w^*}^{0} \frac{\mathrm{d}w}{V_-(w)-\gamma w}. \end{aligned} \]

where \(w_0\) is the initial condition, \(w(0)=w_0\) and \(w^*\) is the local maximum of \(f(w)\).
