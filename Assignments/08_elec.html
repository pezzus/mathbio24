FitzHugh-Nagumo model

Consider the FHN system: \[ \left\{\begin{aligned} \varepsilon v' &= f(v) - w + I_\text{app}, \\ w' &= v - \gamma w, \end{aligned}\right. \]

with \(f(v) = v(1-v)(v-\alpha)\) and \(I_\text{app} \in \mathbb{R}\). We assume that \(\varepsilon \ll 1\), so that we can always apply the singular perturbation analysis.

  1. Show that for \(I_\text{app}>0\) constant and sufficiently large (but not too large), the system has a (singular) limit cycle. Provide a formula for the period.

  2. Try to sketch the bifurcation diagram with respect to \(I_\text{app}\), mentioning all bifurcations.

  3. Anode break excitation is when the potential is slowly decreased (thus, \(I_\text{app}\) is negative rather than positive) and then switched off. Can we trigger an action potential in this way? Does it work also when the application time of the current is short?

Roger-McCulloch model

Consider the Roger-McCulloch system: \[ \left\{\begin{aligned} \varepsilon v' &= -kv(v-\alpha)(v-1) - vw + I_\text{app}, \\ w' &= v - \gamma w, \end{aligned}\right. \]

Suppose \(\varepsilon \ll 1\). The parameters have the same meaning as in the FitzHugh-Nagumo system.

  1. Consider \(I_\text{app}=0\). Study the phase portrait and show how an action potential may occur.

  2. What does it happen when \(I_\text{app}\neq 0\)? Is the situation similar to the previous exercise?

Hodgkin-Huxley model

When using the voltage-clamp technique, the total transmembrane current can be accurately measured. However, Hodgkin and Huxley were interested in the individual sodium \(I_{\ce{Na}}\) and potassium \(I_{\ce{K}}\) currents. How can we estimate them from \(I_\text{tot} = I_{\ce{Na}} + I_{\ce{K}}\)? This exercise addresses the question.

  1. Are there potential levels (fixed by clamping) at which we can measure a pure $ I_{}$ or \(I_{\ce{K}}\) current?
  1. Suppose 90% of the \(\ce{Na+}\) in the bath of a squid axon is replaced by inert choline, preserving electroneutrality. What happens to the equilibrium potentials?
  1. Explain why replacing the extracellular \(\ce{Na+}\) with choline has little effect on the resting potential of an axon. Calculate the new resting potential with 90% of the extracellular \(\ce{Na+}\) removed. Why is the same not true if \(\ce{K+}\) is replaced? Assume \(m = 0.0532\), \(n = 0.3184\), \(h=0.5945\) (constants), \(\bar{g}_{\ce{Na}} = 120\,\text{mS}\), \(\bar{g}_{\ce{K}} = 36\,\text{mS}\), \(\bar{g}_\text{leak} =0.3\,\text{mS}\), \(V_\text{leak} = -54.4\,\text{mV}\) and \(V_{\ce{K}} = -77.3\,\text{mV}\).
  1. Suppose we know \(K = I^1_{\ce{Na}}/I^2_{\ce{Na}}\), where \(I^1_{\ce{Na}}\) is the normal sodium current and \(I^2_{\ce{Na}}\) is the one obtained with 90% of extracellular sodium replaced by choline. Suppose also that \(K\) does not change over time and \(I^1_{\ce{K}}=I^2_{\ce{K}}\). Find \(I^1_{\ce{Na}}\) and \(I^2_{\ce{Na}}\) from \(I^1_\text{tot}\) and \(I^2_\text{tot}\). (A possible way to measure \(K\) is to look at \(I_{\ce{tot}}\) immediately after the voltage clamp, because most of the initial current is carried by sodium.)

  1. Nowadays a simpler way to block channels is with drugs. Tetrodotoxin (TTX) is a powerful neurotoxin, famous for the puffer fish. TTX acts by blocking the sodium channels, hence making them unavailable for action potential formation.

Multidimensional fronts

In excitable media like the cardiac tissue, the cable equation generalises to its multidimensional version: \[ \varepsilon \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = \varepsilon^2 \Delta V + f(V). \]

where \(\Delta\) is the Laplacian, that is for \(x = (x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n)\in\mathbb{R}^n\) we have: \[ \Delta V = \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x_1^2} + \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x_2^2} + \cdot + \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x_n^2}. \]

  1. Show that a planar wave, that is \(V(x,t) = U(\frac{x\cdot\vec{k}+st}{\varepsilon})\) with \(\vec{k}\) the unit vector pointing in the propagation direction, one has exactly the 1-D result for the velocity: \[ s = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(2\alpha-1). \]

  2. Consider now a spherical front, that is \(V(x,t) = U(\frac{|x| + st}{\varepsilon}) = U(r + st)\) where \(r=|x|\) is the radius of the sphere. Using the fast system, show that the speed is as follows: \[ s = s_0 - \kappa \varepsilon, \]

    where \(s_0\) is the speed of a planar front and \(\kappa = \frac{n-1}{r}\) is the curvature of the front. Show that, compared to a planar front, the velocity of a concave front is larger and for a convex front is lower.
