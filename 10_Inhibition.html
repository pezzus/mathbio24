10.1 Competitive inhibition

We have seen that a basic reaction involving enzymes proceeds according to the Michaelis-Menten law: \[ V = \frac{V_\mathrm{max}[\ce{S}]}{K_m + [\ce{S}]}. \]

where \(V\) is the velocity of production.

A very important aspect in the regulation of molecular networks comes from the fact that the presence of an inhibitory molecule may decrease the rate at which an enzymatic reaction occurs. Very schematically, we can think of two main mechanism: competitive inhibition, or allosteric inhibition. In the first case the inhibiting molecule binds to the enzyme in the same site where it binds to the substrate, thus preventing the formation of \(\ce{SE}\) complexes; in the latter case, the inhibiting molecule binds to the enzyme in a different site, but this changes the conformation of the enzyme reducing its affinity to the substrate.

We present now a very simple models of the first case. Letting \(\ce{I}\) represent the inhibitory molecule, can be drawn as \[ \begin{aligned} \ce{S + E &<=>[$k_1$][$k_{-1}$] SE ->[$k_2$] P + E}, \\ \ce{I + E &<=>[$k_3$][$k_{-3}$] IE}. \end{aligned} \]

where \(\ce{SE}\) is the first complex, and \(\ce{IE}\) is a new complex combining the inhibitor and the enzyme.

Let us define \(s = [\ce{S}]\), \(e = [\ce{E}]\), \(i = [\ce{I}]\), \(c_1 = [\ce{SE}]\), \(c_2 = [\ce{IE}]\), and \(p = [\ce{P}]\). The corresponding system is: \[ \left\{\begin{aligned} s' &= - k_1 se + k_{-1}c_1, \\ e' &= - k_1 s e + (k_{-1}+k_2) c_1 - k_3 ie + k_{-3} c_2, \\ c_1' &= k_1 se - (k_{-1}+k_2)c_1, \\ i' &= - k_3 ie + k_{-3}c_2, \\ c_2' &= k_3 ie - k_{-3} c_2, \\ p' &= k_2 c_1. \end{aligned}\right. \]

We can simply the system by noting that: \[ i' + c_2' = 0, \quad\text{and}\quad e' + c'_1 + c'_2 = 0. \]

Since at the beginning \(c_1(0)=c_2(0)=0\), we have \(e = e_0 - c_1 - c_2\) and \(i = i_0 - c_2\). We obtain: \[ \left\{\begin{aligned} s' &= - k_1 s(e_0 - c_1 - c_2) + k_{-1}c_1, \\ c_1' &= k_1 s(e_0 - c_1 - c_2) - (k_{-1}+k_2)c_1, \\ c_2' &= k_3 (i_0 - c_2)(e_0 - c_1 - c_2) - k_{-3} c_2. \end{aligned}\right. \]

Now we proceed with a rescaling. Here we use: \[ x = \frac{s}{s_0}, \quad y_1 = \frac{c_1}{e_0}, \quad y_2 = \frac{c_2}{e_0}, \quad \tau = k_1 e_0 t. \]

The system in the new variables is: \[ \left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{x} &= - x(1-y_1-y_2) + \frac{k_{-1}}{k_1 s_0} y_1, \\ \frac{e_0}{s_0} \dot{y}_1 &= x(1 - y_1 - y_2) - \frac{(k_{-1}+k_2)}{k_1 s_0}y_1, \\ \frac{e_0}{s_0} \dot{y}_2 &= \frac{k_3}{k_1 s_0}\Bigl(\frac{i_0}{s_0} - \frac{e_0}{s_0} y_2\Bigr)(1 - y_1 - y_2) - \frac{k_{-3}}{k_1 s_0} y_2. \end{aligned}\right. \]

We define several constants: \[ \alpha_1 = \frac{k_{-1}}{k_1 s_0}, \quad \alpha_2 = \frac{(k_{-1}+k_2)}{k_1 s_0}, \quad \alpha_3 = \frac{k_{-3}}{k_1 s_0}, \quad \varepsilon = \frac{e_0}{s_0}, \quad \]

so the system reduces to: \[ \left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{x} &= - x(1-y_1-y_2) + \alpha_1 y_1, \\ \varepsilon \dot{y}_1 &= x(1 - y_1 - y_2) - \alpha_2 y_1, \\ \varepsilon \dot{y}_2 &= \frac{k_3}{k_1}\Bigl(\frac{i_0}{s_0} - \varepsilon y_2\Bigr)(1 - y_1 - y_2) - \alpha_3 y_2. \end{aligned}\right. \]

We assume that \(\varepsilon \ll 1\), so \(y_1\) and \(y_2\) are fast. Thus, with the quasi-steady-state approximation we get: \[ \left\{\begin{aligned} 0 &= x(1 - y_1 - y_2) - \alpha_2 y_1, \\ 0 &= \beta (1 - y_1 - y_2) - \alpha_3 y_2, \end{aligned}\right. \]

where \(\beta = \frac{k_3}{k_1}\frac{i_0}{s_0}\). We can solve with respect to \(c_1\) and \(c_2\): \[ \left\{\begin{aligned} y_1 &= \frac{x}{x + \alpha_2 + \frac{\alpha_2}{\alpha_3}\beta}, \\ y_2 &= \frac{\alpha_2}{\frac{\alpha_3}{\alpha_2} x + \alpha_3 + \beta}. \end{aligned}\right. \]

This equilibrium for the fast system is also asymptotically stable, because the Jacobian: \[ J = \begin{pmatrix} -x-\alpha_2 & -x \\ -\beta & -\alpha_3 - \beta \end{pmatrix} \]

has negative trace and positive determinant. Thus, according to Tichonov theorem 9.1, the system quickly converges to the stable equilibrium, and then the slow dynamics kicks in, and its given by the equation for \(x\).

Since the second complex has no product, we are only interested in \(y_1\). Thus: \[ V = [\ce{P}]' = k_2 c_1 = k_2 e_0 y_1 = \frac{k_2 e_0 x}{x + \alpha_2 + \frac{\alpha_2}{\alpha_3}\beta}. \]

Replacing the constants we have: \[ V = \frac{k_2 e_0 \ce{[S]}}{\ce{[S]} + K_m(1 + K_m \frac{i_0}{K_3})} \]

Going back to the production, we have that \[ V = k_2 e_0 y_1 = \frac{V_\mathrm{max} s}{s + K_m (1 + i_0/K_3)}, \]

where \(K_m = \frac{k_{-1}+k_2}{k_1}\) is the half-saturation rate. The effect of the competitive inhibition is to increase the half-saturation rate by a factor \((1+i_0/K_3) > 1\). The Michaelis-Menten curves shifts to the right.

Note that \(i_0 = i + c_2 = i + e_0 y_2\), so \(i_0/s_0 = i/s_0 + \varepsilon y_2\) and hence \(i_0 \approx i\). The variable \(i\) is also slow, like \(s\), we could therefore replace \(i_0\) with \(i = [\ce{I}]\) in the equation: \[ V = \frac{V_\mathrm{max} [\ce{S}]}{[\ce{S}] + K_m (1 + [\ce{I}]/K_3)}. \]

An alternative derivation is to keep the equation for \(i\), rescale it with respect to \(i_0\) and then observe that \(e_0/i_0 \ll 1\). In this case, the system has 2 slow variables, \(i\) and \(s\), and two fast variables, \(y_1\) and \(y_2\).

10.1.1 Example: TTX

Tetrodotoxin (TTX) is a powerful toxin that can quickly kill an adult even at very small dosage. It is a blocker of \(\ce{Na+}\) channels, which are fundamental for impulse transmission in nerves and muscles. The effect of the drug is quite simple: suppose that \([\ce{P}]\) is the number of available channels for correct conduction, \([\ce{B}]\) is the number of blocked channels (thus not transmitting the signal), and \([\ce{D}]\) is the concentration of the drug. Then: \[ \ce{P + D <=>[$k_1$][$k_{-1}$] B}. \]

At equilibrium we have: \[ [\ce{P}][\ce{D}] = K_1 [\ce{B}], \]

and, since \([\ce{P}]+[\ce{B}] = P_0\), the total number of channels, we have: \[ [\ce{P}] = \frac{P_0}{[\ce{D}]/K_1 + 1}, \]

so the presence of the drug reduces by a factor \(([\ce{D}]/K_1 + 1)\) the number of available channels. For TTX, \(K_1\approx \text{1-5 nM}\), so a very small quantity can block most of the channels.

10.2 Allosteric inhibition

Allosteric inhibition is when the inhibitor binds in another site, but can still inhibit the production. The picture is the following

We have several equations to write, and the analysis with QSS approximation can be tedious. We opt here for the quasi-equilibrium approximation. The reactions involved are \[ \begin{aligned} \ce{S + E &<=>[$k_1$][$k_{-1}$] SE ->[$k_2$] P + E}, \\ \ce{I + E &<=>[$k_3$][$k_{-3}$] IE}, \\ \ce{S + IE &<=>[$k_1$][$k_{-1}$] IES}, \\ \ce{I + SE &<=>[$k_3$][$k_{-3}$] IES}. \\ \end{aligned} \]

Assuming all at equilibrium, and taking \(s = [\ce{S}]\), \(e = [\ce{E}]\), \(i = [\ce{I}]\), \(c_1 = [\ce{SE}]\), \(c_2 = [\ce{IE}]\), and \(c_3 = [\ce{IES}]\) we have: \[ \left\{\begin{aligned} k_1 s e &= k_{-1} c_1, \\ k_3 i e &= k_{-3} c_2, \\ k_1 s c_2 &= k_{-1} c_3, \\ k_3 i c_1 &= k_{-3} c_3. \end{aligned}\right. \]

Note that \(K_1 = \frac{k_{-1}}{k_1}\), \(K_3 = \frac{k_{-3}}{k_3}\), and \(e = e_0 - c_1 - c_2 - c_3\). The system becomes: \[ \left\{\begin{aligned} s (e_0 - c_1 - c_2 - c_3) - K_1 c_1 &= 0, \\ i (e_0 - c_1 - c_2 - c_3) - K_3 c_2 &= 0, \\ s c_2 - K_1 c_3 &= 0, \\ i c_1 - K_3 c_3 &= 0. \end{aligned}\right. \]

Remember that\(s\) and \(i\) are fixed, and the unknowns are \(c_1\), \(c_2\), and \(c_3\). The system is linear: \[ \begin{pmatrix} 1 + K_1/s & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 + K_3/i & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -K_1/s \\ 1 & 0 & -K_3/i \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ c_3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} e_0 \\ e_0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \]

and of rank 3, although it is not immediate to see. So we have a unique solution. Note that we are only interested in \(c_1\), and this is easy to find since from the last two equations: \[ c_2 = \frac{K_1}{s} c_3, \quad c_3 = \frac{i}{K_3} c_1, \]

which can be substituted into the first equation to get: \[ (s+K_1)(1 + i/K_3) c_1 = e_0 s, \]

or, in terms of production rate: In this case we obtain: \[ V = k_2 c_1 = \frac{V_\mathrm{max}}{1+[\ce{I}]/K_3}\frac{[\ce{S}]}{K_1 + [\ce{S}]}, \]

which \(V_\mathrm{max} = k_2 e_0\). Again, we find the Michaelis-Menten law, but this time we have a reduction in \(V_\mathrm{max}\) by a factor of \((1+[\ce{I}]/K_3) > 1\).

10.3 Cooperation

Consider the following reaction: \[ \begin{aligned} \ce{S + E &<=>[$k_1$][$k_{-1}$] SE ->[$k_2$] P + E}, \\ \ce{S + SE &<=>[$k_3$][$k_{-3}$] S_2E ->[$k_4$] P + SE}. \end{aligned} \]

The enzyme has 2 (different) sites for binding. When the first complex is formed, there is still room for another substrate, but the rate is different (could be higher, for instance.)

Let us define \(s = [\ce{S}]\), \(e = [\ce{E}]\), \(c_1 = [\ce{SE}]\), \(c_2 = [\ce{S2E}]\), and \(p = [\ce{P}]\). The system is: \[ \left\{\begin{aligned} s' &= - k_1 se + k_{-1}c_1 - k_3 sc_1 + k_{-3}c_2, \\ c_1' &= k_1 se - (k_{-1}+k_2)c_1 - k_3sc_1 + (k_4+k_{-3})c_2, \\ c_2' &= k_3 sc_1 - (k_4+k_{-3}) c_2, \\ p' &= k_2 c_1 + k_4 c_2. \end{aligned}\right. \]

As usual, we have \(e+c_1+c_2=e_0\), so we ignore the equation for \(e\). Note that the equation for \(p\) is different from what we are used to, but still independent from the rest of the system. We have 3 ODEs: \[ \left\{\begin{aligned} s' &= - k_1 s(e_0-c_1-c_2) + k_{-1}c_1 - k_3 sc_1 + k_{-3}c_2, \\ c_1' &= k_1 s(e_0-c_1-c_2) - (k_{-1}+k_2)c_1 - k_3sc_1 + (k_4+k_{-3})c_2, \\ c_2' &= k_3 sc_1 - (k_4+k_{-3}) c_2. \end{aligned}\right. \]

We consider the usual non-dimensionalization: \[ x = \frac{s}{s_0}, \quad y_1 = \frac{c_1}{e_0}, \quad y_2 = \frac{c_2}{e_0}, \quad \tau = k_1 e_0 t, \]

and obtain the following system: \[ \left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{x} &= - x(1-y_1-y_2) + \frac{k_{-1}}{k_1 s_0} y_1 - \frac{k_3}{k_1} x y_1 + \frac{k_{-3}}{k_1 s_0} y_2, \\ \frac{e_0}{s_0} \dot{y}_1 &= x(1-y_1-y_2) - \frac{k_{-1}+k_2}{k_1 s_0} y_1 - \frac{k_3}{k_1} x y_1 + \frac{k_4+k_{-3}}{k_1 s_0} y_2, \\ \frac{e_0}{s_0} \dot{y}_2 &= \frac{k_3}{k_1} x y_1 - \frac{k_4+k_{-3}}{k_1 s_0} y_2. \end{aligned}\right. \]

Defining as usual \(\varepsilon = \frac{e_0}{s_0} \ll 1\), we recognise the fast dynamic in \((y_1,y_2)\) and the slow dynamic in \(x\). The fast system on the \(\eta = \tau/\varepsilon\) time scale reads: \[ \left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d}y_1}{\mathrm{d}\eta} &= x(1-y_1-y_2) - \alpha_1 y_1 - \beta x y_1 + \alpha_2 y_2, \\ \frac{\mathrm{d}y_2}{\mathrm{d}\eta} &= \beta x y_1 - \alpha_2 y_2. \end{aligned}\right. \]

where we introduces some constants, \(\alpha_1 = \frac{k_{-1}+k_2}{k_1 s_0}\), \(\alpha_2 = \frac{k_{-3}+k_2}{k_1 s_0}\), and \(\beta = \frac{k_3}{k_1}\). The equilibrium is: \[ \left\{\begin{aligned} y_1 &= \frac{\alpha_2 x}{\beta x^2 + \alpha_1\alpha_2 + x \alpha_2}, \\ y_2 &= \frac{\beta x^2}{\beta x^2 + \alpha_1\alpha_2 + x \alpha_2}. \\ \end{aligned}\right. \]

Going back to the original variables, we have: \[ \left\{\begin{aligned} c_1 &= \frac{K_{m_2} e_0 s}{K_{m_1} K_{m_2} + K_{m_2} s + s^2}, \\ c_2 &= \frac{e_0 s^2}{K_{m_1} K_{m_2} + K_{m_2} s + s^2}, \end{aligned}\right. \]

where \(K_{m_1} = \frac{k_{-1}+k_2}{k_1}\) and \(K_{m_2} = \frac{k_{-3}+k_4}{k_3}\). The equilibrium is also asymptotically stable in the fast dynamic, since: \[ J = \begin{pmatrix} -(1+\beta)x - \alpha_1 & -x -\alpha_2 \\ \beta x & - \alpha_2 \end{pmatrix}. \]

The Jacobian has negative trace, and the determinant is positive. We can apply Tichonov theorem, so the QSS approximation is valid. The total production rate is: \[ V = k_2 c_1 + k_2 c_2 = \frac{(k_2 K_{m_2} + k_4 s)e_0 s^2}{K_{m_1} K_{m_2} + K_{m_2} s + s^2}. \]

We can have different cases:

  • Case 1: Identical, independent sites, so \(k_1 = 2k_3\), \(k_{-3} = 2k_{-1}\), and \(k_4 = 2k_2\) so: \[ V = 2 \frac{k_2 e_0 s}{K_{m_1} + s}. \]

    This is expected: the sites are independent, so they can work in parallel. The maximum rate is just twice. For \(n\) independent sites, we have the same formula with \(V_\mathrm{max} = n\cdot k_2 e_0\).

  • Case 2: We assume that the first reaction is very slow, but the second is fast. So, \(k_1\to 0\), \(k_3\to\infty\) with \(k_1k_3\) bounded. Therefore, \(K_{m_1}\to \infty\), \(K_{m_2}\to 0\), and \(K_{m_1}K_{m_2}\) bounded. The production rate is: \[ V = \frac{k_4 e_0 s^2}{K_{m_1} K_{m_2} + s^2} = \frac{V_\mathrm{max}s^2}{K_m^2 + s^2}, \]

    where \(K_m^2 = K_{m_1} K_{m_2}\). This function is sigmoidal, very much like Holling type III.

    For \(n\) sites we can prove that: \[ V = \frac{V_\mathrm{max} s^n}{K^n_m + s^n}. \]

    where \(K_m = \prod_{i=1}^n K_{m_i}\). This is an example of a switch-like behavior: at low concentration of \(s\), say \(s\ll K_m\), the production rate is low, otherwise is very high.

10.3.1 Example: hemoglobin

An example is the hemoglobin, which binds oxygen within the blood. The reaction is: \[ \ce{4 O2 + Hb <=> Hb(O2)4} \]

Hemoglobin has 4 sites, but they are not identical, in these sense that after the first oxygen molecule is binded, the second one binds easier, and so more the third and fourth ones.

We can rewrite the reaction as: \[ \ce{O2 + Hb(O2)_{j-1} <=>[$k_j$][$k_{-j}$] Hb(O2)_j} \]

where \(\ce{Hb(O2)_{j}}\) is hemoglobin with \(j\) oxygen molecules attached. Hence, at equilibrium we have: \[ [\ce{Hb(O2)_j}] = \frac{[\ce{Hb(O2)_{j-1}}][\ce{O2}]}{K_j} = \ldots = \frac{[\ce{Hb}][\ce{O2}]^j}{K_1 \cdots K_j} \]

The saturation curve \(Y\) is the amount of hemoglobin bounded to oxygen over the total available one, and it reads: \[ Y = \frac{\sum_{j=0}^4 j [\ce{Hb(O2)_j}]}{4 \sum_{j=0}^4 [\ce{Hb(O2)_j}]}. \]

It is possible to fit \(K_j\) for \(j=1,\ldots,4\) so to match real data, and it is observed that \(K_j\) increases with \(j\).
